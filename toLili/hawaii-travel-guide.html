Six distinct worlds

Choose your island.

Hawaiʻi’s six major visitor islands each have a different personality, landscape and pace. Use the guide to build a trip around what matters to you.

Oʻahu

City energy meets tropical adventure.

HonoluluSurfHistory

Ideal for first trips, families, nightlife, food and a mix of urban and outdoor experiences. Highlights include Waikīkī, Lēʻahi (Diamond Head), Pearl Harbor and the North Shore.

Maui

Golden beaches, volcanic landscapes and scenic drives.

HaleakalāRoad to HānaResorts

A strong fit for beach time, nature, snorkeling and couples. Conditions and access can change; check official updates before visiting specific areas.

Hawaiʻi Island

Big scale, black-sand beaches and volcanic landscapes.

VolcanoesKonaHilo

The largest island offers striking climate variety, Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park, coffee country and beaches. A rental car is often useful.

Kauaʻi

The Garden Isle: lush, dramatic and deeply scenic.

Nāpali CoastHikingWaterfalls

Built around nature: Nāpali Coast, Waimea Canyon, Hanalei Bay and rainforest trails. Weather can change quickly, especially on exposed trails.

Molokaʻi

Rustic charm, quiet roads and a strong sense of place.

Low-keyCultureNature

Best approached with a slower pace and respect for local communities. Services are more limited than on Oʻahu or Maui.

Lānaʻi

Secluded luxury alongside rugged backcountry.

QuietLuxury4×4

A compact island for a slower escape, ocean activities and off-the-beaten-path exploration. Road conditions can make local transport planning important.

Timing matters

There is no single best month for everyone. Think in trade-offs: weather, crowds, prices, surf, hiking and wildlife seasons.

Often a balanced shoulder season. Expect variable rain and generally comfortable conditions.

Warmer, longer-feeling days and generally strong beach appeal. North Shore surf is typically calmer than winter.

Humpback season and famous North Shore surf. Some exposed ocean activities become more hazardous.

Place & people

Travel with respect.

Hawaiian culture is living, diverse and rooted in relationships to land, ocean, family and community.

Aloha • ʻOhana • ʻĀina

Aloha can express love, affection, compassion and connection. ʻOhana refers to family and extended relationships. ʻĀina means land, understood in a broader relationship of care and belonging.

Hula, Hawaiian language, music, lei traditions and food are living cultural practices - not props. Learn from reputable cultural institutions and community voices.

Simple ways to show respect

Stay on marked trails and obey closures.
Do not trespass on private land or restricted places.
Do not touch, chase, feed or harass wildlife.
Follow beach, park and ocean-safety rules.
Support local businesses and community-led experiences.
Leave natural and cultural sites as you found them.
Wild Hawaiʻi

Watch, don’t disturb.

Wildlife encounters are a privilege. Give animals space, follow posted rules and use trained guides when appropriate.

🐢 Green sea turtles

Observe from a respectful distance. Never touch or crowd resting animals.

🐋 Humpback whales

Seasonal visitors. Keep a responsible distance and follow boating rules.

🦭 Monk seals

Critically endangered Hawaiian monk seals need substantial space on beaches.

🐬 Dolphins

Watch wild dolphins without pursuing, feeding or attempting to swim alongside them.

🐦 Native birds

Keep distance, stay on trails and avoid disturbing nesting or feeding areas.

Before a hike or ocean outing

Check weather, surf and park alerts; tell someone your plan; carry water; respect closures; and turn back when conditions become unsafe.

FAQ - should I rely on this page for emergency information?

No. This guide is a planning aid, not a replacement for official emergency services or current government, park, hospital and transportation information. Verify critical information immediately before travel.

What if a trail or attraction is closed?

Do not bypass a closure or barrier. Choose an alternative and check the responsible agency’s current notices.

What about ocean conditions?

Conditions can change quickly. Use lifeguard advice and posted warnings, and choose supervised or guided activities when appropriate.
